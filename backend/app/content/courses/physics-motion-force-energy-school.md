# What is physics? Physical quantities and SI units

**Physics** is the study of matter, energy, and how they interact, from a falling mango to the motion of planets. Physicists make careful **measurements**, look for patterns, and describe them with laws and equations.

## Physical quantities

Anything that can be measured is a **physical quantity**. Every measurement has two parts:

> **Quantity = number × unit**  e.g. length = 5 **m**

Without a unit, "5" means nothing: 5 metres, 5 kilometres or 5 centimetres?

## Fundamental and derived quantities

**Fundamental quantities** are independent and are not defined from other quantities. The International System of Units (**SI**) has seven:

| Quantity | SI unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | metre | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Temperature | kelvin | K |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |

**Derived quantities** are built from fundamental ones:

| Quantity | Formula | SI unit |
|---|---|---|
| Area | length × width | m² |
| Volume | length × width × height | m³ |
| Density | mass ÷ volume | kg/m³ |
| Velocity | displacement ÷ time | m/s |
| Acceleration | change of velocity ÷ time | m/s² |
| Force | mass × acceleration | N (newton) = kg·m/s² |

## Prefixes

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiply by |
|---|---|---|
| mega | M | 10⁶ |
| kilo | k | 10³ |
| centi | c | 10⁻² |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ |
| micro | μ | 10⁻⁶ |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ |

**Examples:** 2.5 km = 2,500 m · 350 g = 0.35 kg · 15 cm = 0.15 m

## Scientific notation

Very large or small numbers are written as **a × 10ⁿ** where 1 ≤ a < 10.

- Distance from Earth to Sun ≈ 150,000,000,000 m = **1.5 × 10¹¹ m**
- Diameter of a hair ≈ 0.00008 m = **8 × 10⁻⁵ m**

## Practice

1. Convert 4.2 km into metres. *(4,200 m)*
2. Convert 750 mg into kilograms. *(7.5 × 10⁻⁴ kg)*
3. Is speed a fundamental or a derived quantity? *(Derived: distance ÷ time.)*

# Measuring instruments and errors

## Common instruments

| Instrument | Measures | Typical precision |
|---|---|---|
| Metre scale | length | 1 mm |
| Vernier calipers | small lengths, diameters | 0.1 mm (often 0.01 cm) |
| Screw gauge (micrometer) | very small thickness | 0.01 mm |
| Beam/electronic balance | mass | depends on model |
| Stopwatch | time | 0.01 s |
| Measuring cylinder | volume of liquids | 1 mL |

## Vernier calipers

A vernier has a **main scale** and a sliding **vernier scale**.

> **Vernier constant (VC) = smallest main-scale division ÷ number of vernier divisions**

If 1 main-scale division = 1 mm and the vernier has 10 divisions, VC = 1 ÷ 10 = **0.1 mm**.

> **Reading = main-scale reading + (coinciding vernier division × VC)**

**Example:** The main scale reads 2.3 cm and the 6th vernier mark lines up exactly with a main-scale mark. With VC = 0.01 cm:
Reading = 2.3 + (6 × 0.01) = **2.36 cm**.

## Screw gauge

> **Pitch** = distance moved by the spindle in one full turn
> **Least count = pitch ÷ number of circular-scale divisions**

If pitch = 1 mm and there are 100 divisions, least count = **0.01 mm**.

> **Reading = linear-scale reading + (circular-scale division × least count)**

## Errors in measurement

- **Systematic error:** always shifts readings the same way, e.g. a **zero error** (the instrument doesn't read zero when it should). Correct it by subtracting the zero error.
- **Random error:** small unpredictable variations. Reduce it by taking **several readings and averaging**.
- **Parallax error:** reading a scale from an angle. Always keep your eye perpendicular to the scale.

## Significant figures

Record only the digits you actually measured, plus one estimated digit. A length measured with a metre scale as 12.4 cm has three significant figures.

**Rules of thumb:** non-zero digits count; zeros between digits count (305 → 3 s.f.); leading zeros don't (0.0042 → 2 s.f.); trailing zeros after a decimal point count (2.50 → 3 s.f.).

## Practice

1. A screw gauge has pitch 0.5 mm and 50 circular divisions. What is its least count? *(0.01 mm)*
2. Five readings of a pendulum's time period are 2.01, 1.98, 2.03, 2.00 and 1.98 s. Find the average. *(10.00 ÷ 5 = 2.00 s)*
3. How many significant figures in 0.06030? *(4)*

# Rest, motion, scalars and vectors

## Rest and motion are relative

An object is in **motion** if its position changes with time **relative to a reference point**, and at **rest** if it doesn't.

A passenger sitting in a moving bus is at rest relative to the bus but moving relative to the roadside trees. So, to describe motion, we must always say *relative to what*.

## Types of motion

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Linear (translational) | along a straight line | a car on a straight road |
| Circular/rotational | around a centre or axis | a ceiling fan blade |
| Periodic | repeats after equal time intervals | a pendulum, Earth's orbit |
| Oscillatory (vibratory) | back and forth about a mean position | a guitar string |

A rolling wheel shows both translational and rotational motion.

## Scalars and vectors

- A **scalar** has magnitude (size) only: mass, distance, speed, time, energy, temperature.
- A **vector** has magnitude **and direction**: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum.

Vectors are drawn as arrows: the length shows the magnitude, the head shows the direction.

## Distance vs displacement

- **Distance:** total path length travelled (scalar).
- **Displacement:** straight-line change in position from start to finish, with direction (vector).

**Example:** Mina walks 30 m east and then 40 m north.

- Distance = 30 + 40 = **70 m**
- Displacement = √(30² + 40²) = √2500 = **50 m**, towards the north-east (north of east).

If she walks around a 400 m track and returns to the start, distance = 400 m but displacement = **0**.

## Adding vectors

- Same direction: add. 5 N east + 3 N east = 8 N east.
- Opposite directions: subtract. 5 N east + 3 N west = 2 N east.
- At right angles: use Pythagoras for the magnitude.

## Practice

1. Classify as scalar or vector: speed, force, time, velocity. *(scalar, vector, scalar, vector)*
2. A boy walks 6 m north then 8 m east. Find the distance and the magnitude of the displacement. *(14 m; 10 m)*

# Speed, velocity and acceleration

## Speed

> **Speed = distance travelled ÷ time taken**  (unit: m/s)

**Average speed** = total distance ÷ total time.

**Uniform speed:** equal distances in equal time intervals. **Non-uniform speed:** unequal distances.

**Example:** A bus travels 120 km from Dhaka to Mymensingh in 3 hours. Average speed = 120 ÷ 3 = 40 km/h.

Converting units: **1 km/h = 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 5/18 m/s**. So 40 km/h = 40 × 5/18 ≈ **11.1 m/s**, and 72 km/h = **20 m/s**.

## Velocity

> **Velocity = displacement ÷ time**  (unit: m/s, with direction)

Velocity changes if the speed **or** the direction changes. A car going round a roundabout at a constant 30 km/h has constant speed but **changing velocity**.

## Acceleration

> **Acceleration a = (v − u) ÷ t**  (unit: m/s²)

- *u* = initial velocity, *v* = final velocity, *t* = time taken.
- Positive acceleration: velocity increases.
- Negative acceleration (**retardation** or deceleration): velocity decreases.
- **Uniform acceleration:** velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals.

**Worked example 1:** A motorbike speeds up from rest to 15 m/s in 5 s.
a = (15 − 0) ÷ 5 = **3 m/s²**

**Worked example 2:** A car moving at 20 m/s brakes to a stop in 4 s.
a = (0 − 20) ÷ 4 = **−5 m/s²** (a retardation of 5 m/s²)

## Practice

1. A cyclist covers 900 m in 3 minutes. Find the average speed in m/s. *(900 ÷ 180 = 5 m/s)*
2. Convert 54 km/h to m/s. *(15 m/s)*
3. A train's velocity changes from 10 m/s to 25 m/s in 30 s. Find the acceleration. *(0.5 m/s²)*
4. Can an object have constant speed but be accelerating? *(Yes, if its direction changes, as in circular motion.)*

# Equations of motion

For motion in a straight line with **uniform acceleration**, four equations connect:

*u* = initial velocity, *v* = final velocity, *a* = acceleration, *t* = time, *s* = displacement.

> 1. **v = u + at**
> 2. **s = ((u + v) ÷ 2) × t**
> 3. **s = ut + ½at²**
> 4. **v² = u² + 2as**

## Where they come from

- (1) comes straight from the definition a = (v − u) ÷ t.
- (2) uses average velocity = (u + v) ÷ 2 for uniform acceleration.
- Substituting (1) into (2) gives (3); eliminating *t* gives (4).

## How to solve problems

1. Write down the three known quantities and the one you want.
2. Choose the equation that contains exactly those four.
3. Keep units consistent (m, s, m/s, m/s²).
4. Take one direction as positive; quantities in the opposite direction are negative.

## Worked examples

**Example 1:** A car starts from rest and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 10 s. Find its final velocity and the distance covered.
u = 0, a = 2, t = 10
v = u + at = 0 + 2 × 10 = **20 m/s**
s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 2 × 10² = **100 m**

**Example 2:** A bus moving at 15 m/s brakes with a retardation of 3 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping?
u = 15, v = 0, a = −3
v² = u² + 2as → 0 = 225 + 2(−3)s → s = 225 ÷ 6 = **37.5 m**

**Example 3:** A train's velocity increases from 10 m/s to 30 m/s while it travels 400 m. Find the acceleration and the time taken.
v² = u² + 2as → 900 = 100 + 800a → a = **1 m/s²**
v = u + at → 30 = 10 + t → t = **20 s**

## Why stopping distance matters

From v² = u² + 2as, the braking distance is proportional to **u²**. Doubling the speed makes the braking distance **four times** as long. This is why speed limits near schools matter so much.

## Practice

1. A bike starts from rest with acceleration 1.5 m/s². What is its velocity after 8 s? *(12 m/s)*
2. How far does it travel in those 8 s? *(48 m)*
3. A car at 25 m/s stops in 50 m. Find its retardation. *(6.25 m/s²)*

# Free fall and motion graphs

## Free fall

When an object falls under gravity alone (ignoring air resistance), it is in **free fall**. All objects fall with the same acceleration, the **acceleration due to gravity**:

> **g ≈ 9.8 m/s²** (downwards, near Earth's surface)

Galileo showed that, without air resistance, a heavy and a light object dropped together land together. On Earth, a feather falls slowly because air resistance is large compared with its weight.

Use the equations of motion with **a = g**:

> v = u + gt  ·  h = ut + ½gt²  ·  v² = u² + 2gh

**Example 1:** A stone is dropped from a bridge and hits the water after 2 s. (u = 0)
Velocity on impact: v = 0 + 9.8 × 2 = **19.6 m/s**
Height: h = ½ × 9.8 × 2² = **19.6 m**

**Example 2:** A ball is thrown straight up at 19.6 m/s. Taking up as positive, g = −9.8 m/s².
At the top, v = 0: 0 = 19.6 − 9.8t → t = **2 s**
Maximum height: 0 = 19.6² + 2(−9.8)h → h = 384.16 ÷ 19.6 = **19.6 m**

## Distance–time graphs

| Shape | Meaning |
|---|---|
| horizontal line | at rest |
| straight sloping line | uniform speed; **slope = speed** |
| curve getting steeper | speeding up |
| curve getting flatter | slowing down |

## Velocity–time graphs

| Shape | Meaning |
|---|---|
| horizontal line | uniform velocity (a = 0) |
| straight line sloping up | uniform acceleration |
| straight line sloping down | uniform retardation |

Two key facts:

- **Slope (gradient) = acceleration**
- **Area under the graph = displacement**

**Example:** A car accelerates uniformly from 0 to 20 m/s in 10 s, then moves steadily at 20 m/s for 5 s.
Acceleration = slope = 20 ÷ 10 = **2 m/s²**
Distance = triangle + rectangle = (½ × 10 × 20) + (5 × 20) = 100 + 100 = **200 m**

## Practice

1. A coin dropped down a well hits the water after 3 s. How deep is the water surface? *(½ × 9.8 × 9 = 44.1 m)*
2. A v–t graph is a straight line from (0 s, 4 m/s) to (6 s, 16 m/s). Find the acceleration and the distance. *(2 m/s²; area = ½ × (4 + 16) × 6 = 60 m)*

# Force and Newton's first law

## What is a force?

A **force** is a push or a pull. It can:

- start a stationary object moving
- stop a moving object
- change its speed or direction
- change its shape

Force is a **vector**, measured in **newtons (N)**.

## Types of force

- **Contact forces:** friction, normal reaction, tension, air resistance, a push.
- **Non-contact forces:** gravitational, magnetic, electrostatic.

## Fundamental forces of nature

1. **Gravitational force:** the weakest, but with infinite range; holds planets in orbit.
2. **Electromagnetic force:** between charges; responsible for almost all everyday contact forces.
3. **Strong nuclear force:** the strongest; holds the nucleus together, very short range.
4. **Weak nuclear force:** involved in radioactive (beta) decay.

## Balanced and unbalanced forces

- **Balanced:** the forces cancel out (net force = 0). The motion doesn't change.
- **Unbalanced:** there is a net force, so the motion changes (the object accelerates).

## Newton's first law (law of inertia)

> An object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, **unless an unbalanced external force acts on it**.

**Inertia** is the tendency of an object to resist changes to its motion. **More mass means more inertia.**

- **Inertia of rest:** when a bus starts suddenly, standing passengers fall backwards, because their bodies tend to stay at rest.
- **Inertia of motion:** when a bus brakes suddenly, passengers lurch forward, because their bodies keep moving.
- Shaking a tree makes fruit fall: the branch moves, the fruit tends to stay at rest and breaks away.
- Seat belts provide the force that stops you during a sudden stop.

## Practice

1. Why is it harder to push a loaded van than an empty one? *(More mass, so more inertia.)*
2. Dust comes out of a carpet when it is beaten. Explain. *(The carpet moves; the dust, due to inertia of rest, stays behind and separates.)*

# Newton's second law and momentum

## Momentum

> **Momentum p = mass × velocity = mv**  (unit: kg·m/s, a vector)

A slow, heavy truck and a fast cricket ball can both have large momentum.

**Example:** A 1,000 kg car at 20 m/s has p = 1000 × 20 = **20,000 kg·m/s**.

## Newton's second law

> The rate of change of momentum is proportional to the net force applied and takes place in the direction of the force.

For constant mass this becomes the famous equation:

> **F = ma**

**1 newton** is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s².

## Worked examples

**Example 1:** What force is needed to accelerate a 50 kg box at 2 m/s²?
F = 50 × 2 = **100 N**

**Example 2:** A force of 600 N acts on a 1,200 kg car. Find the acceleration.
a = F ÷ m = 600 ÷ 1200 = **0.5 m/s²**

**Example 3:** A 0.15 kg cricket ball moving at 30 m/s is caught and stopped in 0.1 s. Find the average force on the fielder's hands.
Change in momentum = 0.15 × (0 − 30) = −4.5 kg·m/s
F = change in momentum ÷ time = −4.5 ÷ 0.1 = **−45 N** (45 N, opposite to the ball's motion)

## Impulse

> **Impulse = F × t = change in momentum**

For the same change in momentum, a **longer time means a smaller force**. That's why:

- fielders pull their hands back when catching
- vehicles have crumple zones and airbags
- high jumpers land on soft mats

## Mass and weight

- **Mass** (kg): the amount of matter; the same everywhere.
- **Weight** (N): the gravitational force on the mass.

> **W = mg**

A 50 kg student weighs 50 × 9.8 = **490 N** on Earth. On the Moon (g ≈ 1.6 m/s²), the same student weighs about 80 N, but the mass is still 50 kg.

## Practice

1. Find the momentum of a 60 kg runner moving at 8 m/s. *(480 kg·m/s)*
2. A 2,000 N force acts on a 500 kg object. Find its acceleration. *(4 m/s²)*
3. Find the weight of a 12 kg bag. *(117.6 N)*

# Newton's third law and conservation of momentum

## Newton's third law

> **For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.**

Forces always come in pairs. If A pushes B, then B pushes A with a force that is:

- equal in size
- opposite in direction
- acting on **different** objects (so they don't cancel each other out)

## Examples

- **Walking:** your foot pushes the ground backwards; the ground pushes you forwards.
- **Rowing a boat:** the oars push water backwards; the water pushes the boat forwards.
- **Rockets:** the engine pushes hot gas out downwards; the gas pushes the rocket up.
- **Firing a gun:** the bullet goes forward and the gun **recoils** backwards.
- **Jumping from a boat** to the shore pushes the boat away from the shore.

## Conservation of momentum

> If no external force acts on a system, its **total momentum stays constant**.

> **m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂**

This follows from the third law: during a collision, the forces on the two objects are equal and opposite and act for the same time, so the momentum one gains equals the momentum the other loses.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 (recoil):** A 4 kg gun fires a 0.02 kg bullet at 400 m/s. Find the gun's recoil velocity.
Before firing, total momentum = 0.
0 = (0.02 × 400) + (4 × v) → 4v = −8 → v = **−2 m/s** (2 m/s backwards)

**Example 2 (objects stick together):** A 1,000 kg car moving at 10 m/s hits a stationary 1,500 kg car and they move together.
1000 × 10 + 1500 × 0 = (1000 + 1500)v → v = 10,000 ÷ 2,500 = **4 m/s**

## Practice

1. Why does a heavier gun recoil less than a light one firing the same bullet? *(Same momentum, larger mass, so smaller velocity.)*
2. A 50 kg boy jumps from a 100 kg boat at 2 m/s towards the shore. Find the boat's velocity. *(0 = 50 × 2 + 100v → v = −1 m/s, 1 m/s away from the shore)*

@lesson Friction | 20
# Friction

**Friction** is a force that opposes the relative motion (or attempted motion) between two surfaces in contact. It arises because surfaces are rough at a microscopic level and their particles attract each other.

## Types of friction

| Type | When it acts | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Static friction | the object is not yet moving | adjusts up to a maximum (limiting friction) |
| Sliding (kinetic) friction | one surface slides over another | less than limiting static friction |
| Rolling friction | an object rolls (wheel, ball) | much smaller than sliding |
| Fluid friction (drag) | moving through liquid or gas | increases with speed |

So it's harder to **start** pushing a heavy almirah than to keep it moving, and much easier to move it on rollers.

## Factors affecting friction

- **Nature of the surfaces:** rougher surfaces give more friction.
- **Normal force:** pressing the surfaces together harder gives more friction.

## Friction is useful

- Walking without slipping
- Brakes stopping a vehicle
- Writing with a pen or pencil
- Tyre grip on the road
- Lighting a matchstick

## Friction is a problem

- It wears out shoes, tyres and machine parts.
- It wastes energy as heat, lowering efficiency.
- It reduces the speed of vehicles, increasing fuel use.

## Reducing friction

- **Lubricants** (oil, grease) in engines and bicycle chains.
- **Ball bearings** turn sliding into rolling.
- **Polishing** surfaces.
- **Streamlined shapes** for cars, aeroplanes, boats and fish reduce drag.

## Increasing friction

- Treads on tyres and shoe soles.
- Sand on slippery roads.
- Chalk powder on gymnasts' hands.

## Friction and Newton's laws

When you push a box at a constant velocity, your push **equals** the friction force: the forces are balanced, so acceleration is zero (first law). If the push is greater than friction, the net force accelerates the box: **F_net = F_push − friction = ma**.

**Example:** You push a 20 kg box with 100 N and friction is 60 N.
Net force = 40 N, so a = 40 ÷ 20 = **2 m/s²**.

## Practice

1. Why do tyres have treads? *(To increase grip, and to channel water away on wet roads.)*
2. A 10 kg trolley is pulled with 50 N against 20 N of friction. Find its acceleration. *(3 m/s²)*

# Work

In physics, **work** is done when a force moves an object in the direction of the force.

> **W = F × s**  (force × displacement in the direction of the force)

Unit: **joule (J)**. 1 J = 1 N × 1 m: work done when a force of 1 N moves an object 1 m.

## When is no work done?

- You push a wall with all your strength but it doesn't move: s = 0, so **W = 0** (although you feel tired, because your muscles use energy internally).
- You carry a bag horizontally at constant height: the force (upwards) is perpendicular to the motion (sideways), so **no work** is done on the bag by your lifting force.

## Force at an angle

If the force makes angle θ with the motion:

> **W = F s cos θ**

- θ = 0°: W = Fs (maximum)
- θ = 90°: W = 0
- θ = 180°: W = −Fs (negative work, e.g. friction opposing motion)

## Work against gravity

To lift a mass *m* through height *h* at constant speed, you apply a force equal to its weight *mg*:

> **W = mgh**

## Worked examples

**Example 1:** A boy pushes a box with a 40 N force for 5 m. W = 40 × 5 = **200 J**

**Example 2:** A labourer lifts a 25 kg sack onto a 2 m high truck. W = 25 × 9.8 × 2 = **490 J**

**Example 3:** A child pulls a toy cart with 10 N along a rope at 60° to the ground for 8 m. W = 10 × 8 × cos 60° = 10 × 8 × 0.5 = **40 J**

## Practice

1. How much work is done lifting a 2 kg book 1.5 m onto a shelf? *(29.4 J)*
2. A satellite moves in a circular orbit. Does gravity do work on it? *(No: the gravitational force always points to the centre, at 90° to the motion.)*

# Kinetic and potential energy

**Energy** is the capacity to do work. It is a scalar, measured in **joules (J)**.

## Forms of energy

Mechanical (kinetic and potential), heat, light, sound, electrical, chemical, nuclear and magnetic energy. In this course we focus on **mechanical energy**.

## Kinetic energy (KE)

The energy an object has **because it is moving**:

> **KE = ½mv²**

**Derivation:** A force F accelerates mass *m* from rest to velocity *v* over distance *s*.
Work done = Fs = ma × s. From v² = u² + 2as with u = 0, as = v²/2.
So W = m × v²/2 = **½mv²**, and this work is stored as kinetic energy.

Because KE depends on **v²**, doubling the speed **quadruples** the kinetic energy. This is why high-speed crashes are so destructive.

**Example:** A 1,000 kg car at 20 m/s has KE = ½ × 1000 × 20² = **200,000 J** (200 kJ).

## Gravitational potential energy (PE)

The energy an object has **because of its position** above a reference level:

> **PE = mgh**

**Example:** A 5 kg pot on a 2 m high shelf: PE = 5 × 9.8 × 2 = **98 J**

## Other stored energy

- A stretched rubber band or compressed spring stores **elastic potential energy**.
- Food, fuel and batteries store **chemical energy**.
- Water held behind a dam stores gravitational potential energy, which the Kaptai hydroelectric power station converts into electrical energy.

## Practice

1. Find the KE of a 0.5 kg ball moving at 10 m/s. *(25 J)*
2. Find the PE of a 40 kg child at the top of a 3 m slide. *(1,176 J)*
3. Two cars have the same mass; one is twice as fast. Compare their kinetic energies. *(The faster car has 4 times the KE.)*

# Conservation of energy

> **Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only change from one form to another. The total energy of an isolated system stays constant.**

## Energy transformations

| Device or event | Transformation |
|---|---|
| Solar panel | light → electrical |
| Electric fan | electrical → kinetic (+ heat, sound) |
| Battery torch | chemical → electrical → light (+ heat) |
| Hydroelectric dam | gravitational PE → kinetic → electrical |
| Rubbing hands | kinetic → heat |
| Photosynthesis | light → chemical |

## Mechanical energy in free fall

Without air resistance, **KE + PE stays constant**. As an object falls, PE decreases and KE increases by the same amount.

**Example:** A 2 kg mango falls from a 5 m high branch.

| Position | PE = mgh | KE | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top (h = 5 m, at rest) | 98 J | 0 J | 98 J |
| Halfway (h = 2.5 m) | 49 J | 49 J | 98 J |
| Just before the ground (h = 0) | 0 J | 98 J | 98 J |

Speed just before hitting the ground: ½mv² = mgh → v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 5) = √98 ≈ **9.9 m/s**. Notice that the mass cancels: every object dropped from 5 m lands at the same speed (without air resistance).

## A swinging pendulum

At the highest points: maximum PE, zero KE. At the lowest point: minimum PE, maximum KE. In reality air resistance and friction slowly convert mechanical energy into heat, so the swing dies down. The energy isn't destroyed; it spreads out as heat.

2. With what speed does it hit the ground? *(√(2 × 9.8 × 20) = √392 ≈ 19.8 m/s)*
3. Where does the "lost" mechanical energy go when a bouncing ball bounces lower each time? *(Into heat and sound.)*

# Power and efficiency

## Power

**Power** is the rate of doing work or transferring energy.

> **P = W ÷ t**  (unit: **watt, W**; 1 W = 1 J/s)

- 1 kilowatt (kW) = 1,000 W
- **1 horsepower (hp) ≈ 746 W**

Two students climb the same stairs. They do the same work if they have the same mass, but the one who climbs faster has **more power**.

**Example 1:** A 50 kg student runs up stairs 6 m high in 10 s.
Work = mgh = 50 × 9.8 × 6 = 2,940 J
Power = 2,940 ÷ 10 = **294 W**

**Example 2:** A water pump lifts 600 kg of water to a 10 m tank in 2 minutes.
Work = 600 × 9.8 × 10 = 58,800 J; t = 120 s
Power = 58,800 ÷ 120 = **490 W**

## Electrical energy and your bill

Electricity companies charge per **kilowatt-hour (kWh)**, also called a "unit":

> **Energy (kWh) = Power (kW) × time (h)**

1 kWh = 1,000 W × 3,600 s = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

**Example:** A 100 W fan runs 10 hours a day for 30 days.
Energy = 0.1 kW × 10 h × 30 = **30 kWh (30 units)**

## Efficiency

No machine converts all its input energy into useful output; some is always "wasted", usually as heat.

> **Efficiency = (useful output energy ÷ total input energy) × 100%**

**Example:** A motor uses 2,000 J of electrical energy and does 1,500 J of useful work.
Efficiency = 1,500 ÷ 2,000 × 100% = **75%**. The other 500 J becomes heat and sound.

Efficiency can never be 100% for a real machine.

## Saving energy

LED bulbs are far more efficient than filament bulbs: more of the electrical energy becomes light and less becomes heat. Choosing efficient appliances saves money and reduces fuel burned in power stations.

## A five-step method for any problem

1. **Read** carefully and draw a sketch.
2. **List** the known quantities with units, and the unknown.
3. **Convert** to SI units (km/h → m/s, g → kg, minutes → s).
4. **Choose** the formula containing your knowns and the unknown; substitute.
5. **Check** that the answer has the correct unit and a sensible size.

## Mixed practice (answers below)

1. A car moving at 72 km/h brakes uniformly and stops in 5 s. Find (a) its retardation and (b) the stopping distance.
2. A 2 kg object is pushed with 30 N against 10 N of friction. Find its acceleration and its velocity after 4 s from rest.
3. A 60 kg person stands in a lift. What is their weight?
4. A 3 kg ball moving at 4 m/s hits a stationary 1 kg ball. The 3 kg ball continues at 2 m/s. Find the velocity of the 1 kg ball.
5. A stone is dropped from a 45 m high building. Find the time to reach the ground and its speed on impact (ignore air resistance).
6. A 1,200 kg car accelerates from 10 m/s to 20 m/s. How much work did the engine do (ignore friction)?
7. A motor of efficiency 80% lifts a 100 kg load 8 m in 10 s. Find its useful output power and its input power.
8. **Conceptual:** Why do cricketers wear helmets with padding, and why do boxers' gloves have thick padding? Use impulse to explain.

## Answers

1. u = 20 m/s, v = 0, t = 5 s → (a) a = −4 m/s² (retardation 4 m/s²); (b) s = ((20 + 0)/2) × 5 = **50 m**
2. Net force = 20 N → a = **10 m/s²**; v = 0 + 10 × 4 = **40 m/s**
3. W = 60 × 9.8 = **588 N**
4. 3 × 4 + 0 = 3 × 2 + 1 × v → v = **6 m/s**
5. 45 = ½ × 9.8 × t² → t² ≈ 9.18 → t ≈ **3.03 s**; v = √(2 × 9.8 × 45) = √882 ≈ **29.7 m/s**
6. W = ΔKE = ½ × 1200 × (20² − 10²) = 600 × 300 = **180,000 J**
7. Useful work = 100 × 9.8 × 8 = 7,840 J; output power = **784 W**; input power = 784 ÷ 0.8 = **980 W**
8. Padding increases the time over which the momentum changes during an impact. Since F = change in momentum ÷ t, a longer time means a **smaller force** on the head or face.
